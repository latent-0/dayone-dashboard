The four bets
  • 1. Outbound first. Inbound is empty, so a hand-built list of named US and European sponsors and CEOs, contacted on a 21-day, 5-email + 3-LinkedIn cadence, is the engine for the first 90 days.
  • 2. Partner-led LinkedIn warms the list. Three partner profiles post three times a week so the first email is never from a stranger.
  • 3. One capture path that ends in a diagnostic. Value Map sample, scorecard, briefing, scoped diagnostic. Every form captures organisation, role and country.
  • 4. Be citable by AI assistants. Fix the site basics so ChatGPT, Gemini and Perplexity can find and quote Dayone when sponsors ask who the operating partners are.

What this means
  • There is no inbound pipeline to optimise yet. Optimising conversion on an empty funnel wastes the first 30 days.
  • Generic contact forms invite generic outreach. A form that asks for organisation, role and country, and sits behind a specific offer, filters itself.
What it does not mean
  • It does not mean sponsors cannot be reached. It means nobody has asked them yet, in a way they can say yes to.
  • The Day One Technologies site fix (pre-rendering) shows the team can move fast on site work: its score rose from 70 to 83 in days.
Consequences for the plan
  • Outbound leads the first 90 days; content supports it.
  • The Ventures site basics are a day-1 task, not a later one.
  • Every asset gates on organisation, role and country.
  • Success is measured on briefings, not form fills.

2 · Who we go after, and whereDayone Ventures

A narrow list of named accounts, in tiers.

Dayone’s own principle is “a narrow mandate, run deep”. The go-to-market follows it: a short list we can research properly beats a long list we cannot. Geography is the United States and Europe; India and Nigeria are excluded everywhere.

Tier A · Sponsors

PE firms, independent sponsors, search funds and family offices that back lower-middle-market software ($5M–$40M ARR, €5M–€40M).

  • Buyer: Partner, Principal, Operating Partner, VP Portfolio Operations
  • Why they buy: a hundred-day plan that needs owners; a portfolio company stuck flat
  • Volume: ~60% of the list

Tier B · Portfolio management

CEO, CFO or COO of a sponsor-backed software company at the same size.

  • Why they buy: NRR below target, margin pressure, a board asking for a bigger number
  • Route in: direct, and through their sponsor as the second contact
  • Volume: ~25%

Tier C · Founders and owners

Owners of software or tech-enabled businesses weighing a control or structured partner.

  • Why they buy: pre-exit in 12–24 months, growth plateaued, want a higher multiple
  • Route in: content, introductions, advisers
  • Volume: ~15%
Where: region plan (suggested split, review at day 35)
United States · ~60%New York, Boston, Chicago, Texas, SF Bay, Atlanta, Philadelphia / NJ. Contact 8–10am local, Tue–Thu.
Europe · ~40%UK and Ireland, DACH, Benelux, Nordics, France. B2B outreach on legitimate interest with a one-click opt-out; keep a record of the basis.
ExcludedIndia Nigeria accounts and contacts, on every list and in every tool.
How the list is built (week 1–4, ~150 accounts: ~90 US, ~60 Europe)
  • Start from sponsor websites and portfolio pages: who owns lower-middle-market software?
  • For each portfolio company, record ARR band, stage, sponsor, location and the contact.
  • Add one specific, true observation per account (retention, pricing, a recent hire, an acquisition). No observation, no send.
  • Verify every address before sending. Tag each account Tier A, B or C and US or Europe.
Trigger signals worth a message now
  • New acquisition or add-on closed
  • New CEO or CFO appointed
  • Sponsor announces a new fund or deployment
  • Company in a 12–24 month hold window
Qualify before the briefing (scored in the dashboard)
  • ARR band and sponsor identified
  • Which of the six value dimensions hurts most
  • Who owns the number after we leave
  • Hold or exit horizon
Not our ICP
  • Pre-revenue, consumer hardware, non-software
  • Advice or slides only, no operating accountability
  • Vendors, list sellers, agencies, resellers
  • Buyers headquartered in India or Nigeria

The metric hierarchy
  • North star: sponsor briefings held per month.
  • Leading indicators: positive replies per week, keyword comments per week, ICP captures per week.
  • Efficiency: reply rate, positive-reply rate, briefing rate, diagnostic rate, cycle length in days.
  • Health: bounce rate under 2%, complaints under 0.1%, audit score above 85.
  • Pipeline: declared budget of qualified inbound, and diagnostics started.

Targets are not set now. They are set at the day-35 read-out from measured rates, then reviewed monthly.

7 · Roles, rhythm and risksDayone Ventures

Someone owns it, weekly.

Roles (names to be assigned)
  • Partner sponsor: owns the plan and the ICP; runs briefings.
  • Growth owner: runs outbound, the list, the weekly log and the weekly meeting.
  • Three posting partners: three posts a week, ten comments a day.
  • Site and ops: site fixes, forms, domains, the dashboard.
  • Design (as needed): carousels and the two free assets.
Weekly growth meeting · 30 minutes · same day each week
  • Read the Growth tracker: last week’s numbers against the plan.
  • Look at every briefing and every reply: what worked?
  • Pick the one variable to change this week.
  • Confirm next week’s 20 accounts and 3 posts.
  • Update the 90-day checklist.
Tool categories (choose, then budget)
  • Sending: 2 lookalike domains, 2–3 mailboxes, a sequencing tool with bounce handling.
  • Data: list verification and a source for sponsor portfolios.
  • LinkedIn: a paid profile tier for search, plus the posting partners’ own accounts.
  • Booking and forms: a booking link, the sheet already in use.
  • Measurement: this dashboard; GA4 and Search Console to add.

Costs are not quoted here: they depend on the tools chosen.
